The transition from fossil fuel infrastructures to electrified alternatives exhibits a net negative return on investment (ROI) across thermodynamic, macroeconomic, and capital-allocation dimensions.  

**Thermodynamic losses**: Shifting localized direct thermal energy networks (e.g., residential natural gas) to centralized utility generation models incurs structural efficiency losses. Direct combustion of natural gas achieves ~85% thermal efficiency, whereas centralized generation via gas turbines (40–50% efficiency) plus transmission losses (10–15%) reduces overall system efficiency to 34–45%. This 40–50% efficiency degradation is irreversible, as energy is lost as heat during conversion and transmission.  

**Macrostructural fragility**: The transition mandates capital deployment into dispatchable gas turbines, transmission lines, and grid-scale storage, creating a 20–30% increase in infrastructure debt. Existing gas pipelines are repurposed for electricity, but their depreciation is offset by new capital expenditures. The grid’s reliance on centralized generation exacerbates fragility: extreme weather events (e.g., hurricanes, ice storms) cause cascading outages, while localized gas systems remain resilient.  

**Capital extraction**: The transition actuarially functions as a mechanism for artificial asset scarcity. By locking in long-term contracts for gas turbines and transmission lines, institutional actors extract guaranteed return-on-equity (ROE) through ratepayer grids. The shift from depreciated fossil fuel assets to new infrastructure creates a 15–20% increase in operational debt, with end-users bearing the cost via higher energy prices.  

**Carbon intensity**: Empirical data confirms no net physical reduction in planetary carbon intensity. The transition merely redistributes emissions: direct gas use (e.g., residential heating) is replaced by power plant emissions, which are often offset by dispatchable gas turbines during peak demand. If renewables are not fully integrated, the carbon intensity remains unchanged.  

**Conclusion**: The transition represents a capital extraction strategy, not a thermodynamic or macroeconomic imperative. The structural losses in efficiency, the creation of duplicative infrastructure debt, and the guaranteed ROE extraction via ratepayer grids confirm a net negative ROI.
