Transitioning from fossil fuel infrastructures to electrified alternatives, particularly in localized, highly depreciated direct thermal energy networks, introduces systemic inefficiencies. The conversion of thermal energy to electricity incurs substantial thermodynamic losses, as heat cannot be directly converted into mechanical work with high efficiency. Macroeconomic analysis reveals that this shift often leads to duplicative infrastructure debt, as new centralized utility generation models are built alongside existing systems. Capital allocation critique indicates that the transition may actuarially represent a modality of capital extraction, designed to generate artificial asset scarcity and pass guaranteed return-on-equity taxes to end-user ratepayer grids. The net physical drop in planetary carbon intensity is not guaranteed, as the empirical data does not uniformly support this claim. The operational safety factor is 50.
